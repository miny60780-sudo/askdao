Decisions: facing a choice you cannot see around

Move or stay. Accept or decline. Speak now or wait. The hardest decisions share a shape: the information is incomplete, both paths cost something, and not choosing is also a choice.

First, separate the decision from the fear of it

Under pressure, a decision can feel like a judgment on your whole future. It rarely is. Most choices are smaller than they feel at midnight: many can be adjusted, some can be reversed, and almost all of them look different after one honest conversation or one more piece of information. Naming what is actually irreversible in the choice — and what is merely uncomfortable — shrinks it back to its real size.

An older way of looking at it

The I Ching is, at its core, a book about timing. Again and again its texts distinguish between acting and ripening: the same step that is right in one season is a mistake in another, not because the step changed, but because the moment did. Its questions are therefore rarely "which option is correct?" They are closer to: What is actually being asked of me now? What happens if I move early, and what happens if I move late? Which part of this am I trying to force — and which part is still genuinely undecided in the world, not just in my mind? A casting will not make the decision for you. It can slow the moment down enough for you to see which decision is really yours.

Three questions worth sitting with

What do I know for certain, and what am I only imagining — in both directions?

Which part of this choice is irreversible, and which part can I revisit?

What answer am I hoping for — and would I trust it if it arrived?
