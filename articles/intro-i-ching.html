A Beginner's Guide to the I Ching

The I Ching (易经) — also called the Zhou Yi (周易), the Book of Changes — is one of the oldest classics still in continuous use. Its subject is change.

The system reads any situation by breaking it into two polarities and working from those. What follows is a plain introduction to the structure, and to how a casting is actually read.

Where it comes from

The received text divides into two parts. The jing (经) is the core: the judgement and the line statements for sixty-four hexagrams. The zhuan (传), the "Wings" — ten short commentaries known collectively as the Ten Wings (十翼) — explain them. Traditional attribution gives the original hexagrams to King Wen of Zhou, the eight trigrams to Fu Xi, and the Wings to Confucius. Modern scholarship is less generous: the core looks Western Zhou in origin, the Wings were written over the Warring States and Han periods, and the received text differs from early manuscripts: the Mawangdui silk manuscript (c. 168 BCE) and the Guodian bamboo slips (c. 300 BCE) preserve visibly different versions.

Two lines, then three

A yang line is drawn solid; a yin line is drawn broken. Three lines stacked make a trigram (八卦). There are eight:

  • Qian ☰ — heaven, three unbroken lines
  • Dui ☱ — lake
  • Li ☲ — fire
  • Zhen ☳ — thunder
  • Xun ☴ — wind
  • Kan ☵ — water
  • Gen ☶ — mountain
  • Kun ☷ — earth, three broken lines

Stack two trigrams, one above the other, and you have a hexagram (卦). Eight times eight gives sixty-four, each with its own name, image and judgement: Qian 乾 "the Creative," Kun 坤 "the Receptive," Zhun 屯 "Difficulty at the Beginning," Zhong Fu 中孚 "Inner Truth," Jing 井 "the Well." They are normally listed in the King Wen sequence, the running order credited to King Wen of Zhou and used in received editions, which reads as a narrative — difficulty at the start, then progress, decline and return. A second ordering, the Fu Xi sequence, arranges them by the binary structure of the lines so that a hexagram and its opposite sit across from each other. That symmetry is what caught the eye of the German polymath Leibniz, who recognised in it the binary arithmetic he was working on and presented a paper on binary notation to the Paris Academy of Sciences in 1703.

Casting

The method in general use today is three coins, tossed six times. Heads count three and tails two, and the sum gives each line. A total of 6 is an old yin (changing), 7 a young yang (stable), 8 a young yin (stable), 9 an old yang (changing). Six tosses give six lines, built from the bottom up. This coin method is the popular one; the classical method described in the Great Commentary uses fifty yarrow stalks, and the coin tradition is a later convenience that Zhu Xi recommended in the Song for its simplicity.

Reading the result

A casting gives you at least two hexagrams. The primary hexagram (本卦) is the situation as it stands. If any line came out changing — a 6 or a 9 — flip it and you get the changed hexagram (变卦), read as the direction the situation is heading. A third, the nuclear hexagram (互卦), is built from the middle lines — the second, third and fourth making its lower trigram, the third, fourth and fifth its upper — and describes what lies inside the situation. The commentary tradition reads a hexagram through its judgement (彖), its image (象) and the statement attached to each line, then weighs the moving lines against one another.

Traditional Chinese practice adds najia (纳甲), which assigns each line a five-element relation to the day and month, and the "six relatives" (六亲) — parent, sibling, child, wife-wealth, officer-ghost — read according to which element strengthens or weakens them. It is a layered interpretive scheme rather than part of the ancient core, and practitioners still work this way.

What kind of book it is

Read one way, the I Ching is a philosophical classic on the nature of change, cited by poets, statesmen and physicians for two millennia. Read another way, it is a divination practice with a technical method. Most serious readers use both lenses at once.
