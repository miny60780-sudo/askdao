Winter Solstice 冬至: Dumplings, Tangyuan and the Count of Nines

The shortest day of the year in the northern hemisphere has long been marked as a beginning rather than merely a low point — and for much of Chinese history it was the larger of the two year-end celebrations.

Dongzhi (冬至) is the twenty-second of China's twenty-four solar terms, falling when the sun reaches its southernmost point, around 21–23 December — the moment the days begin, almost imperceptibly, to lengthen again.

The older new year

For much of Chinese history the winter solstice was the larger of the two year-starts. Under the Zhou calendar the year began with the solstice month itself; the Qin and early Han began the year in the tenth month (early winter) instead, until the Han Taichu calendar of 104 BCE adopted the Xia reckoning, starting the year at the second new moon after the solstice (usually). Custom kept the emphasis long after the calendar moved: a saying still common in southern China holds that the winter solstice is "as important as the new year" (冬至大如年). In the Ming, the Hongwu emperor reportedly made the festival a three-day state holiday.

The imperial state marked the day with a formal offering at the Temple of Heaven in Beijing — the business of the court, and history rather than a service offered here. AskDao publishes cultural material only and provides no religious services, rites or blessings.

Dumplings in the north

Northern Chinese families gather to wrap dumplings (饺子) together on the solstice, and the wrapping is usually the point: a table of bowls, a rolling pin, and fillings that nobody quite agrees on. The standard northern filling is pork and cabbage, but shrimp, chive with egg, and lamb with cumin are all common. Dumplings also carry small good-luck items — a coin, a piece of ginger — and the crescent shape is traditionally linked to the idea of sealing the body's warmth in against the cold.

The usual story behind them names Zhang Zhongjing, the Eastern Han physician, as the inventor: he saw his neighbours lose their ears to frostbite and made ear-shaped dumplings for them to eat. It is a legend attached to a well-known figure rather than a documented event. Southern relatives of the dumpling are wontons (馄饨), now standard in Cantonese and Sichuan cooking.

Tangyuan in the south

In much of southern China the solstice instead means tangyuan (汤圆): glutinous rice balls, usually filled with black sesame or peanut paste, served in syrup. The same round ball is eaten at the Lantern Festival, where it is more often called yuanxiao (元宵), and the two holidays share a reason — the sphere stands for reunion (团圆), which is what a family gathers to keep at the year's darkest point. The contrast is a broad tendency rather than a hard border: plenty of northern households eat tangyuan, and plenty of southern ones make dumplings too.

Counting the nines

Another solstice custom is counting in nines (数九). From the winter solstice, days are grouped into nine periods of nine, and people watch what the weather does at each stage. The verses went into print widely, in a form most readers will recognise:

One nine and two nines — hands stay in your sleeves;
Three nines and four nines — you walk on the ice;
Five nines and six nines — watch the willows along the river;
Seven nines the river opens, eight nines the geese arrive;
Nine nines and one more, and the ploughing oxen are everywhere.

The count runs eighty-one days from the solstice, ending in mid-March, around the Jingzhe (惊蛰) and Spring Equinox (春分) terms. In older households children marked each day on a "nine-nine frost-dispelling picture" (九九消寒图) — a grid of plum blossoms, or eighty-one characters to be filled in one by one — until the ninth nine ended and the cold was declared over.

Also on the day

Cleaning the house beforehand, setting out food for ancestors at a household shrine, and eating a plain dish called winter solstice rice (冬至饭) are observances of the season in different regions. The character of the day varies too: some households keep it quiet and domestic, some treat it as the year's first real holiday after the long autumn.
