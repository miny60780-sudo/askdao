Culture · Ideas

Two States Are Enough

A claim now circulating online holds that the tai chi diagram is, in effect, the source code of the modern computer — that large language models were already sketched in an ancient book. Stated that strongly, it will not survive scrutiny. But the pattern it points at is real, it is older than any machine, and it rewards a careful look.

The pattern is this: take a distinction with exactly two sides, combine it in sequence, and you can encode a world. The I Ching did it with lines. Life does it with bases. Computing does it with switches.

A unit that can only be two things

The atom of the I Ching is the yao (爻), a single line that comes in two forms only: solid for yang, broken for yin. Stack six of them and you have a hexagram. Two choices, made six times, give exactly sixty-four figures — no more, no fewer — and the classic is built from all of them.

The Song-dynasty scholar Shao Yong arranged the sixty-four in a circular diagram ordered by their line structure. Read a solid line as 1 and a broken line as 0, and his arrangement counts, in order, from 0 to 63. It is a complete combinatorial system — every possible six-place sequence of two symbols, laid out a thousand years ago as an instrument for contemplating change. The German polymath Leibniz, sent Shao Yong's diagram from Beijing by the Jesuit Joachim Bouvet, recognised his own binary arithmetic in it and published his paper on binary notation in 1703; our beginner's guide tells that story in full.

The circle is a calendar

The lines were never only counters. Han-dynasty scholars singled out twelve hexagrams — the "sovereign hexagrams" (十二消息卦) — in which yang lines wax from the bottom up and then wane again, and matched them to the twelve months. At one end stands Fu 復, a single yang line returning beneath five yin: the winter solstice, light at its weakest and already turning. At the other stands Qian 乾, six yang lines complete: the summer solstice. And precisely at that peak, the sequence turns — the first yin line appears at the base of the next figure, Gou 姤. Yang at its fullest already carries the seed of yin inside it.

Join the twelve in a ring and refine the count to the twenty-four solar terms, and the boundary between the dark half and the light half of the ring bends into the famous S-curve, each half swelling where the other thins, each holding a dot of the other at its widest point. This is worth dating honestly: the interlocking "fish" diagram everyone recognises is a late refinement, Ming dynasty in its familiar form. Zhou Dunyi's eleventh-century Taijitu was a set of concentric circles with no fish in it at all. The yin–yang idea is close to three thousand years old; its most famous picture is only a few hundred. Read through the calendar, what the finished diagram draws is not two fish. It is one year.

Life writes in pairs, too

In 1953 the structure of DNA was worked out: a double helix, two strands wound around a ladder of paired bases. There are four bases — adenine, thymine, guanine, cytosine — but they are organised as two complementary pairs, A with T and G with C. The pairing is strict enough that one strand completely specifies the other; that is how a cell copies some three billion base pairs at every division, and how a reader who is shown half the molecule already knows the other half.

It is often noticed — a recent wave of short videos has made much of it — that a double helix viewed along its axis, or a spiral pressed flat onto a page, traces a curve much like the tai chi diagram's dividing line. This should be labelled for what it is: a visual parallel, not a derivation. No biologist takes the helix from the diagram. What genuinely connects the two is structural rather than pictorial. In the tai chi figure, each half carries the other's information too: lift away the black and the white that remains still has the black's exact shape along every curve. Complementarity — one side fully implying the other — is the shared idea, in a Song-dynasty diagram and in the molecule alike.

And the machine

A transistor is a switch with two states, conducting or not. Everything a computer does, however elaborate, is assembled from vast numbers of these, and from numbers written in base two — the binary arithmetic Leibniz published in 1703. A large language model is, at its visible level, a network of weighted connections passing signals onward or letting them die; at the level beneath, it is arithmetic performed by two-state switches, nothing else. Peel the machine down far enough and two values remain: 1 and 0. Connected, or broken. On, or off. The resemblance to a solid line and a broken one is not something anyone designed into the machine. It is what is left when any system is reduced to the sharpest distinction it can make.

What the pattern does not prove

It does not prove that ancient Chinese scholars anticipated genetics or computing. Two-state systems appear wherever a distinction is drawn as sharply as possible — day and night, open and shut, whole and broken — and similar reductions have been found more than once in history. The honest claim is narrower and, in the end, more interesting. The authors of the I Ching tradition took that single distinction earlier than anyone else we know of, and worked it out more completely: sixty-four figures, a calendar, a philosophy of change, all generated from two kinds of line. The Daodejing states the programme in one sentence: "The Tao gives birth to One. One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to the ten thousand things." The hexagrams are that sentence carried out in arithmetic — two states, combined without remainder, until they are sufficient to describe a changing world.
