Sources & Approach

How this site works, and where its words come from

Our texts

The hexagram judgments and images on AskDao are contemporary summaries written for this site. They are informed by James Legge's public-domain translation, The Yî King (1882) — a Victorian-era rendering with its own period flavour — but they are not a scholarly translation. For study of the original text, consult the Chinese Zhouyi itself or a modern academic translation. The eight trigrams' nature and family images follow the Shuogua (說卦) commentary; those images are a traditional symbolic system, not claims about how families should be. Quotations from the Daodejing and Zhuangzi are cited by chapter where they appear.

How we read

The I Ching began, in early China, as a divination text, and its line statements speak frankly of good and ill fortune. We choose to read it as a mirror, not a verdict. That is a modern way of reading — our choice, stated openly — not the book's only tradition. Wuxing (五行) we present as the Five Phases: patterns of change and relation, not five material "elements". Wuwei (無為) we render as "action without forcing", not "doing nothing". A changed hexagram is never presented as a forecast.

Psychology, in its place

AskDao brings together the traditional perspective of the I Ching and thoughtful insights from modern psychology. The I Ching provides the symbolic framework; psychology informs how we create space for reflection. They are distinct traditions, brought together as complementary ways of exploring life's questions. We do not claim the I Ching has been scientifically validated, and we do not offer diagnosis, counseling, or therapy.

What we deliberately do not do

No predictions. No good- or ill-fortune verdicts on your life. No religious services. No claim to read your subconscious. Your question is sent only to generate your reading; it is not stored or shared.
